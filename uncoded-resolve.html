Case study 01 · Simultaneous-Turn Tactics UI

Uncoded Resolve: a tactics game you can read at a glance

In Uncoded Resolve, you and the enemy pick your moves at the same time, then watch them play out together. Every turn is a read on your opponent, so the screen has to show a lot at once without burying the fight.

Role
Solo designer and developer: game design, UI, 2D art, icons, logos
Timeline
Apr to Sep 2026, mostly one 10-week course (UCSB ECE 184)
Tools
Paint.net, Photoshop, Blockbench, Unity UI Toolkit, DaVinci Resolve
Outcome
Playable gold master for Windows and Mac, with a trailer and press kit
Team & credits
Solo project. 3D character models and animations commissioned from Filbyte; dialogue help from Devin Leung. Built in UCSB ECE 184, taught by Professor Pradeep Sen.
Under the hood

Architecture, tests, and readable enemy AI

I specified the game's C# architecture, subsystem requirements, and acceptance criteria, then used coding agents to implement against those specs. I gated changes with 307 automated Unity Test Framework tests across 39 suites.

For enemy decisions, I designed a hybrid of per-personality utility rules and a PPO reinforcement-learning policy trained with PyTorch, exported to ONNX for inference in Unity. The goal was an opponent that played well while remaining understandable to the player.

The challenge

Two sides plan at once, and the player has to see all of it

Each combat turn, the player chooses an action for their fighter while the enemy secretly does the same. To choose well, the player has to keep track of health, stamina, and energy for every unit, status effects on both sides, three lanes of tiles, and a bench of teammates they can swap in.

How do I put all of that on screen without covering the two characters the player actually cares about?

01 · Prototype

Prove the fun before drawing anything

By week 3 I had combat running as a text-only program in the terminal. That told me the simultaneous-turn idea was fun before I spent a single hour on art. From there I moved it into Unity with capsules as stand-ins, then kept adding art in stages.

I also balanced the core actions (attack, defend, charge, rest, swap, and maneuver) with a spreadsheet of weights and outcome values. I went through the possible action combos to make sure no single move was obviously the best choice.

02 · Layout

Mocking up the combat HUD

I started from bounding boxes laid over the real camera angle, blocking out where each piece of information would live. Your team's bench sits on the left and the enemy's on the right. Actions cluster at the bottom right, and the turn counter sits top center, which leaves the middle of the screen for the fight. I looked at Minecraft Dungeons and Honkai: Star Rail for how other games handle similar systems.

Something to read about the enemy

A Hostile Analysis card tells the player what an enemy is built to do: its stats, its ability, and its tactic (this one "prefers safe counters"). Simultaneous turns reward reading your opponent, so the game gives you something to read.

I made this card in Paint.net, working over an AI-generated image that I used as a reference.

03 · Visual language

A color and symbol system that tells you who's who

With several characters and two factions on screen, color had to carry meaning, not decoration. I gave every side its own color and used it everywhere that side appears: health bars, tiles, icons, and dialogue.

Icons: from rough mocks to one family

I drew quick mocks of each action first, then redrew the whole set so every icon reads as part of one family. Status effects like stagger and invisibility share the same style, so players learn one visual vocabulary.

04 · The usability miss

The tutorial nobody could close

The first playable build taught combat with tip panels that closed only through a small X in the corner. I thought it was clean. Playtesters didn't agree: nobody knew to press the X, so they got stuck on the very screens meant to help them. In my post-mortem, I called it one of the worst design choices I've ever made.

I rebuilt the tutorial around the same idea: guide the player through every step. It now blocks every action except the one it's teaching and shows one short tip per step, so there's never a moment where the player doesn't know what to do next.

"A good tutorial is unnoticed." My rule now: build the tutorial alongside each mechanic, and never hide a control behind an unlabeled X.

05 · Final build

The finished game

The gold master has two playable levels, an exploration mode with objectives, terminals, a minimap, and branching persuasion dialogue, plus save/load and level select. Getting simultaneous actions to feel responsive took about 40 animation variants.

Reflection

What I'd do differently

  1. Onboarding is core product work. I left the tutorial and sound for the last two weeks, and both suffered. They now get built alongside the mechanics.
  2. Size the space to the story. The dialogue ran long for such small maps. Next time I'd plan each map around the story beats it has to hold.
  3. Take one big risk per project. I spent three weeks getting a reinforcement-learning enemy AI to work. A simpler utility AI would have left that time for playtesting.
